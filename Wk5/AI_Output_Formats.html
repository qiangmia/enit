🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

  1. Describe four different output formats you can ask an AI for, and say when each one is best.
  2. Use vocabulary for AI output, formats and human oversight.
  3. Recognise future forms in real writing and use them to talk about what AI will do.
  4. Explain in their own words why the human job changes when AI does the work.

📄 Reading: Four Ways to Ask the Same Question

Where this comes from. Andrej Karpathy is a computer scientist who works on artificial intelligence. On 2 October 2026 he posted a short message on X (formerly Twitter) about how we should work with AI. This text is a simplified and shortened adaptation written for this course, with only a few short phrases quoted directly.

Read the original post →

Karpathy begins with a prediction. He thinks that people will spend much more time in the future trying to understand what AI produces. Because of that, he gives some practical tips — and all of them are about the format, not the facts.

1. Ask for plain, controlled English

His first suggestion is to ask the AI to explain something in ASD-STE100. This is a real kind of controlled language. It was created for writing aircraft maintenance manuals, where a badly written sentence can stop a plane. Its rules are very strict: short sentences, no special words, and the active voice only.

The result is not beautiful writing, but it is much easier to read. If the rules feel too strict for your purpose, Karpathy suggests asking for "80% of the way" to that style — which is itself a useful trick, because it shows you can set the rules rather than simply accept them.

2. Ask for a diagram instead of text

Instead of asking for paragraphs, ask for a diagram. A good diagram explains a structure faster than a page of words, because your eyes find the shape of something before they read the small details of it.

Example. To understand how a packet travels through a network, one diagram showing the router, the switch and the server may be clearer than five paragraphs describing the same path.

3. Ask for HTML, and get a real web page

This is his most useful tip for students. If you ask for your answer "in HTML", the AI can return an actual web page — with buttons, colour and animation — instead of plain text that you have to imagine.

Example. Instead of a list of twenty glossary words, ask for a page where each word flips over to show its meaning when you click it. You can open the result in a browser and use it like a small app.

4. Ask for an explainer video

Karpathy thinks this will be the most important format of all. He suggests asking for a "3b1b-style" explainer: a short animated video that teaches one idea well. He expects AI to produce these quickly for any topic a person asks about.

What this means for us

In the last part of the post, Karpathy makes two points, and this is the part that matters most for your own career.

First, your job will change shape. As AI gets better, it will do more and more of the legwork. So the human job will move. We will spend less time producing things and more time checking them and understanding them. Karpathy says our work will "rise up the abstractions" — we move to a higher, more abstract level, where the job is guiding and checking instead of typing.

Second, this is good news. AI can also help with the checking. Karpathy points out that we can now ask for large, bespoke, discardable software — a tool built for one small problem and thrown away afterwards. Before AI, building a special program for a single question was never worth the time. Now it can be. His advice is to push the boundaries: try things you would never have tried before, and you will be surprised.

A small warning. These are one expert's tips on one day. The post does not claim that HTML or video is always better — sometimes plain text is exactly what you want, and a diagram is useless for an opinion. Karpathy's real advice is to experiment with formats and compare them. That is a study skill as much as a technical one.

📚 Grammar Link: Future Forms (Week 5 Focus)

The post is full of future forms. Here they are, with what each one actually does.

Form Example from the post Use it for
will + base "LLMs will do more of their legwork." a prediction about the future
will be + -ing "We will be spending a lot more time understanding." an arrangement you have already decided
present simple "As LLMs get better…" a general truth, like a zero conditional
if + present → will "If you ask for HTML, you will get a web page." a result that always happens

Try it. Finish these two sentences about your own studies:
• "In five years, I think I will be working …"
• "If I ask my AI for a diagram , it will show …"

⚠️ Six phrases that will block you

These are the hardest parts of the original post. Tap each one to open it.

Literal meaningAbstractions are general ideas, and "rising up" means moving to a higher level.

In contextAs AI takes over the detailed work, people move to a higher level: guiding and checking.

Try it: "As software does more work, our job rises up the abstraction level."

Literal meaningThe heavy lifting at a construction site.

In contextThe boring, repetitive part of a job — copying, sorting, retyping.

Try it: "Nobody enjoys the legwork of renaming fifty files."

MeaningTo believe something will succeed or be valuable. A business word, from "bull" = rising strongly.

In contextKarpathy is "bullish on" explainer videos — he thinks they will matter most.

Try it: "I'm bullish on this course." (Plain English: "I'm confident in this course.")

MeaningTo try to go beyond what is usual or normally allowed.

In contextDo not stay inside what is normal. Make the unusual request.

Try it: "We pushed the boundaries by asking for a video instead of an essay."

MeaningSomething you can throw away after using. The opposite of durable (long-lasting).

In contextSoftware built for one question and then deleted. Cheap to make now, so not worth saving.

Try it: "A discardable script for a one-time calculation."

MeaningAn idiom. Most, but not all, of a quality you are describing.

In contextThe full ASD-STE100 style is too strict, so ask for a slightly softer version.

Try it: "Write in formal English — about 80% of the way to a legal document."

📚 Vocabulary: Six Terms to Keep

Study these, then cover the definitions and test yourself.

output
输出糟果
The result an AI gives you back.
"The output was a web page, not a paragraph."
format
案式
The way information is arranged or presented.
"Same facts, different format."
controlled language
受控語詢
A strict standard for technical writing where every word must be easy to understand.
"Aircraft manuals use controlled language."
diagram
图表、示意图
A picture showing how parts relate to each other.
"A diagram explained the network faster than a page of text."
legwork
瀔撹資功的工作
The boring, tiring part of a job that has to be done anyway.
"AI will take over the legwork."
oversight
监眶自勤橋檢核
Checking someone's work carefully to make sure it is correct.
"Our job shifts to oversight."

🔧 Activity: The Four-Format Test (15 minutes)

This is the useful part. Actually try the advice on a topic from this week.

  1. Pick one idea from the Week 5 lesson — for example variables, loops, or debugging.
  2. Ask an AI to explain it four times: as plain text, as a diagram, as an HTML page, and as a storyboard for a 60-second video.
  3. Score each version 1–5 for clarity, accuracy and usefulness for revising.
  4. Be a sceptic: check one fact from each version against the lesson or a reliable source, and note any that are wrong.
  5. Bring your table to class, ready to say which format you would use and why — and which one you would not trust.

Step 4 is the point of the exercise. Karpathy is describing a way to get more out of AI, not a way to avoid checking it. A beautiful web page can still be completely wrong. Format improves how well you absorb an answer; it never tells you whether the answer is true.

🏠 Wrap-up
  • Format matters: plain text → diagram → HTML → video.
  • You can set the rules, not just accept them ("80% of the way").
  • AI takes the legwork; people take the oversight.
  • More formats mean more choices — and more reason to check.
