This week: A routine deployment goes wrong at three in the morning. You will read an incident timeline from a cloud operations team, learn how engineers describe what happened without naming who did it, and write the three sentences of a status update.

Before you read: A website stops working at 3 a.m. Who should tell the users what happened — and how much should they say?

🎯By the end of this week, you will be able to
  • explain what a region, a replica and a failover are;
  • read a timeline and work out cause and effect in an outage;
  • use the passive voice to describe what was done;
  • write three status-page sentences that are accurate and calm;
  • explain what a post-mortem is for.

Read: The 3 a.m. Rollout

Read along: tap ▶ for narration. Tap a highlighted word for its definition.

On Thursday at 02:50, Lin Yu-Ting started a routine rollout. She is 24 and works on the cloud operations team of a software company in Taipei. The new routing rule had been tested in the staging environment, and two engineers had signed it off. It was pushed to the Singapore region first, because that region carries the least traffic at that hour.

At 03:04 the first alert arrived. Requests to one availability zone were timing out. The dashboard showed healthy servers, which made no sense: the machines were running, but traffic was not reaching them.

Yu-Ting opened the routing table. The new rule sent all traffic to the older zone, where the replica was already under heavy load. The replica was not down. It was saturated: still running, but unable to answer fast enough. Latency climbed for four minutes before anyone noticed.

At 03:11 she rolled the change back. Normal traffic returned at 03:19. The status page was updated once, and it was three sentences long: “A routing change was rolled back. Service was restored at 03:19. No data was lost.” The downtime was fifteen minutes: customers in the affected zone saw an error page, and everyone else noticed nothing.

On Friday morning the team held a post-mortem. Nobody was blamed. The question was why the staging test had passed. The answer was simple: it had been run against one region, and the problem only appeared when two regions were compared.

Yu-Ting wrote the report in the passive on purpose. “When something breaks at three in the morning,” she says, “the last thing users need is a name. They need to know what happened, when it will be fixed, and whether their data is safe.”

📚Vocabulary: running a service

These words appear in the article. Read the definition and the example, then find the word in the text.

region
區域		One large area of a cloud provider’s network, such as Singapore or Frankfurt.The rule was pushed to the Singapore region first.
availability zone

Useful pattern: “Failover means moving traffic to a working copy. For example, when the first region slows down, traffic is sent to the second one.”

📐Grammar: The Passive Voice (present and past)

The passive moves the thing that was acted on to the front of the sentence. Incident reports and status pages use it because the action matters more than the person.

FormExample
is / are + past participleTraffic is routed through two regions.
was / were + past participleThe change was rolled back at 03:11.
has / have been + past participleThe rule has been removed.
will be + past participleA new test will be added next week.
modal + be + past participleThe status page must be updated immediately.

Key rules

  • The object moves to the front and the verb becomes be + past participle: Two engineers signed off the change → The change was signed off by two engineers.
  • Use it when the action matters more than the actor: status pages, incident reports, procedures.
  • Use it when the actor is unknown (No data was lost) or obvious from the context.
  • Add by + actor only when the actor is the point of the sentence.
  • Keep the tense. A past event stays past: was rolled back, not is rolled back.

Common mistakes

The most common error is dropping be: ✗ The rule rolled back at 03:11. → ✓ The rule was rolled back at 03:11.

The second error is using the passive to hide responsibility. “Mistakes were made” is grammatically correct and useless in a report: say who did what, or say plainly that nobody knows.

Show answers

  2. is routed
  3. was lost

Activity B: Write a status update

Your service has been down for twelve minutes because a configuration change broke the login page. Write three status-page sentences: what happened, what was done, and what happens next. Use the passive twice and the active once.

✓ A configuration change was deployed at 09:12. Login requests could not be completed. The change was rolled back at 09:24, and the service is being checked now.
🔁Rephrasing

Changing between active and passive is a rephrasing move. The meaning stays; the focus moves.

Active → passive
Original: Two engineers signed off the change.
Rephrased: The change was signed off by two engineers.
Passive → active
Original: The change was rolled back at 03:11.
Rephrased: Yu-Ting rolled back the change at 03:11.
