Read: What we know about the global Microsoft outage

Tap a highlighted word for its definition.

On Thursday evening, Microsoft 365 stopped working for users around the world. Teams, Outlook and Copilot were hit by what Microsoft called “connectivity problems”. Many people could not send email, open files or check their calendar. Some could still use Teams chat and meetings.

Microsoft said the outage was not a cyber-security incident. Its first attempts to fix the problem did not work. On Friday morning, the company reported only “marginal success”.

More than 430 million people use Microsoft 365. The full scale of the outage has not been confirmed. The monitoring site Downdetector received 140,000 reports about Outlook alone, from 74 countries. The real number is probably much higher.

The outage did not stay inside Microsoft. OpenAI and Anthropic, two AI companies, reported problems. The Singaporean government and Australia’s spy agency said they were affected. In the UK, doctors’ practices asked patients to phone instead of sending email.

What caused it? Microsoft ruled out an attack and pointed to elevated load. A large number of servers became unexpectedly unavailable, the company said. Traffic moved to the remaining servers, and they could not handle it.

Microsoft said its fixes were “not taking as expected”. It was monitoring activity across regions and looking for ways to improve capacity. The company did not say when the service would be fully restored.

Experts say the effects reach further than one company. “Microsoft is a massive part of the internet backbone,” said Troy Hunt, an Australian security expert. “It reminds us how critical email still is.” Paul Spain, of Gorilla Technology, said companies had practically “lost a business day”.

The cause was not an attack. It was capacity: too few servers, too much traffic on the rest, and fixes that did not work. The knock-on effect reached governments, hospitals and companies that do not run Microsoft’s systems — they simply depend on them.

Adapted from “What we know about the global Microsoft outage”, BBC News, 13 November 2026 (bbc.com). Simplified for classroom use.

📐Grammar: the passive in an incident report

Reports use the passive when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or already obvious. The thing that was affected becomes the subject.

FormExample
present: am / is / are + past participleReports are collected by Downdetector.
past: was / were + past participleTeams, Outlook and Copilot were affected.
present perfect: has / have been + past participleThe full scale has not been confirmed.
past perfect: had been + past participleThe rule had been tested in staging.
modal: must / can + be + past participleThe cause must be found before the post-mortem.

Key rules

  • The object becomes the subject: Someone moved the traffic → The traffic was moved.
  • Keep the tense: They moved it → It was moved, not It is moved.
  • Only transitive verbs take the passive. The service went down has no passive form.
  • On a status page the passive is normal: users need the state, not the blame.
  • In a post-mortem, name the actor. Mistakes were made hides the one fact readers need.

Common mistakes

✗ The service was went down. → ✓ The service went down. (go down is intransitive)
✗ The cause has been confirm. → ✓ The cause has been confirmed.
✗ The rule was roll back. → ✓ The rule was rolled back.

Activity B: active or passive?

Choose the better sentence for the situation, and say why in one sentence.

  1. Status page: “We broke it.” / “The service was disrupted at 03:04.”
  2. Post-mortem: “Mistakes were made.” / “The team pushed an untested rule.”
  3. News report: “The outage was caused by human error.” / “An engineer applied the rule to the wrong region.”
  4. Alert: “Traffic was moved to the second region.” / “We moved traffic to the second region.”
Show suggested answers
  1. The passive. Users need to know what is happening, not who to blame.
  2. The active. A post-mortem exists to name what happened and who did it.
  3. The active is more precise. The passive is acceptable only if the actor is genuinely unknown.
  4. Either. The passive fits an automatic failover; the active fits a decision by the team.
🔁Rephrasing

The same event, two audiences. A status page protects users from detail; a post-mortem protects the team from repeating the mistake.

Blame → status
Original: We broke it.
Rephrased: The service was disrupted at 03:04.
Vague → accountable
Original: Mistakes were made.
Rephrased: The team skipped the staging check.

Try it

  1. Mistakes were made.
  2. The outage was caused by human error.
  3. We lost a business day.
Show model answers
  1. The team skipped the staging check.
  2. An engineer applied the rule to the wrong region.
  3. Companies lost a business day of email and payments.
