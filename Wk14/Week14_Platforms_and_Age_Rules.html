This week: The UK is banning social media for under-16s, and every paragraph of the story is somebody speaking: the government, the regulator, the platforms. You will learn to track who said what, and how the reporting verb tells you whether the writer believes it.

Before you read: If a government bans an app for teenagers, who should check the user’s age: the app, the phone maker, or the parent?

🎯By the end of this week, you will be able to

Read: Who Checks the Age?

Read along: tap ▶ for narration. Tap a highlighted word for its definition.

The UK government has announced a ban on social media for under-16s. The apps named so far are Snapchat, TikTok, YouTube, Instagram, Facebook and X. Australia has gone further: its list also includes Kick, Reddit, Threads and Twitch.

Messaging apps such as WhatsApp and Signal are not expected to be included, and gaming sites such as Roblox sit in an unclear middle.

A company says; a regulator tells; a professor warns; a minister claims.

The closing line of the report

The details matter, because a ban needs an exemption list and a way to check ages. The government says it will use “highly effective age assurance”, a term that covers facial age estimation, photo ID matching and digital identity services. The regulator, Ofcom, will be expected to enforce it.

Not everyone is comfortable. YouTube said it was “a vital resource for young people” and argued that a ban risked pushing children towards “anonymous, less safe services”.

Ofcom told the government that checks at 16 are harder than checks at 18, because there is less identity data to compare. Roblox said it had already expanded its own tools to estimate a user’s age.

Then there is the question of bypassing. A VPN can disguise a user’s location, and some children already use one. The children’s minister said there were “options” for age-gating VPN use. The prime minister said it was inevitable that some teenagers would try to get around the rules, but that this did not mean the legislation was wrong.

The timetable is tight. The government said it wanted a vote by the end of 2026 and the rules in force early in 2027. A professor of internet law warned that secondary legislation can be challenged in the courts, which could delay it.

Meanwhile, Australia’s early results are not encouraging: one survey found that 61% of 12–15-year-olds who had used restricted platforms still had access to at least one account.

Read the verbs and you can hear the argument. A company says; a regulator tells; a professor warns; a minister claims. Reported speech is not just grammar — it is how a news story keeps its distance from what people want you to believe.

📚Vocabulary: rules and enforcement

📐Grammar: Reported Speech and Reporting Verbs

News writing turns what people said into reported speech. The tense moves back, the pronouns change, and the reporting verb carries an opinion.

FormExample
said (that) + clauseYouTube said (that) it was a vital resource.
told + person (that)Ofcom told the government (that) checks at 16 were harder.
warned / argued / claimed / deniedA professor warned that the law could be challenged.
said + would / could / wasThe minister said the ban would come into force in 2027.
according to + personAccording to Ofcom, the checks are harder at 16.

Key rules

  • Move the tense back one step: is → was, will → would, can → could, has → had.
  • Change pronouns and time words: today → that day, next year → the following year, here → there.
  • Say does not take a person object; tell does: said that… / told the government that…
  • The reporting verb carries a stance: claim suggests doubt, warn suggests danger, deny answers an accusation.
  • If the fact is still true, the tense can stay: Ofcom says the checks are harder at 16.

Common mistakes

Forgetting the tense shift is the standard error: ✗ YouTube said that it is a vital resource. → ✓ YouTube said that it was a vital resource.

And tell needs a person: ✗ Ofcom told that checks were harder. → ✓ Ofcom said that checks were harder. / ✓ Ofcom told the government that checks were harder.

🔁Rephrasing

Turning a quote into reported speech is rephrasing at its most useful: same information, no quotation marks, and a clear distance from the claim.

Direct → reported
Original: “The ban will start in 2027.”
Rephrased: The government said the ban would start in 2027.
Quote → source
Original: “It is a vital resource.” — YouTube
Rephrased: According to YouTube, the site is a vital resource.

Try it

  1. “Checks at 16 are harder.” — Ofcom
  2. “Children may move to less safe apps.” — YouTube
  3. “I want a vote this year.” — the minister
Show model answers
  1. Ofcom said that checks at 16 were harder.
  2. YouTube said that children might move to less safe apps.
  3. The minister said that she wanted a vote that year.
