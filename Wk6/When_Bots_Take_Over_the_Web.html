🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

  1. Explain in their own words what an AI agent is, and how it differs from a chatbot.
  2. Describe three problems that AI agents create — for websites, for businesses and for users.
  3. Use modals of obligation (must, mustn't, have to, don't have to, should, be supposed to) to write rules.
  4. Practise the four question types used in the Week 8 mid-term: reading, rephrasing, vocabulary and grammar.

📄 Reading: When Bots Take Over the Web

Where this comes from. This is a simplified and shortened adaptation of a BBC Future article by the technology writer Thomas Germain. He spent a week testing Meta's new AI agent, "Muse". Only a few short phrases are quoted directly; the rest has been rewritten for this course.

Read the original BBC article →

1. A helper with a browser

Last month, Meta released a new AI agent called Muse. An agent is different from a chatbot. A chatbot answers your questions; an agent goes out into the world and does things for you. Muse has its own web browser. It can cancel your gym membership, argue about a price with customer service, order your groceries, and invite your friends to a party. Soon, it will be able to make phone calls, too.

Muse is the first free, full-featured agent from a big technology company, and millions of people downloaded it in the first few weeks. Other companies are building agents as well. To do its job, Muse asks for a great deal of trust: it wants to read your email and your calendar, and on some computers it asks to control the whole machine.

This sounds convenient. But the experts who spoke to the BBC say it is the start of a difficult period for the internet. The web was designed for humans, who have limited time and patience. AI agents have neither.

2. The "Swiftie problem"

In 2024, fans of the singer Taylor Swift found that concert tickets in the United States were extremely expensive. Many of them flew to Europe instead, where the same tickets cost much less. Ticket scalpers — people who buy tickets only to resell them at a higher price — had taken most of the seats before real fans could.

Now imagine that every fan has a personal scalper robot. Calli Schroeder, who works for a privacy group in the United States, predicts that this problem will "explode". When you try to book a table at a popular restaurant, or renew a passport in the busy season, you may find that agents have already taken every slot. Because booking is effortless for a machine, some people will even hoard appointments — booking five "just in case".

3. Things fall apart

The trouble does not stop at tickets. If one agent sends 600 enquiries to a small business while an ordinary person might send two, the business is buried under machine-made messages. Robots do not look at advertisements and do not buy subscriptions, and many websites depend on exactly that money.

For years, traffic from bots has quietly grown. One study found that bot traffic rose by more than 120% in the year to June 2026 — roughly nine times faster than traffic from real people. Some websites are already struggling to stay online under the load.

4. Will the robots betray you?

There are personal risks, too. Schroeder says that if you let an agent plan your holiday, you cannot easily check whether it found the best price — or a flight that pays its own company a fee instead. She examined Muse's terms of service and says they do not promise that the agent will act in your interest.

Security experts also found serious flaws in the Muse app within days of its release. Meta says the problem was fixed immediately, and that Muse had been delayed for months to make it safer. But the researcher who found the flaw said the mistakes were so easy to find that he could not trust the company's promises.

5. The rules of the road

Not everyone is pessimistic. Ramesh Raskar, a professor at MIT, says we need "rules of the road".

"Imagine there are no rules of the road, and you release billions of cars — the AI agents — and you just let them drive through playgrounds. That's where we are." — Ramesh Raskar, MIT

Raskar suggests treating the AI models as engines and the agents as cars, and then writing real traffic rules for them: windshields, brakes and traffic lights. Some of that is already beginning. A few restaurant booking platforms have banned people who misuse AI. The UK rewrote the rules for booking driving tests, partly to fight bots. And some websites are now testing a "pay-per-crawl" system that charges AI programs a fee when they collect the site's data.

The road ahead will be bumpy, and users will pay a price in personal data. But the internet was rebuilt for smartphones once before — with apps, mobile pages and touch screens. It can be rebuilt for agents, too.

A note on balance. This article looks mainly at the problems, because that is what the experts were asked about. Meta argues that Muse is safe and that users stay "in charge". Keep both sides in mind when you discuss the questions below.

📚 Grammar Link: Modals of Obligation (Week 6 Focus)

The article is full of rules: what agents should do, what websites must not do, and what is simply not necessary. Here are the modals from the Week 6 lesson, with examples from this article.

FormWhat it meansExample
must + basea strong rule, often a written one"Agents must follow a site's rules."
have to + basea strong rule coming from the situation"Websites have to handle far more traffic."
should + baseadvice, not a rule"An agent should act in your interest."
be supposed to + basean expectation, softer than must"Agents are supposed to ask before buying."
mustn't + baseit is forbidden"Websites mustn't sell your private data."
don't have to + baseit is not necessary"You don't have to accept every default."

Watch out. mustn't (forbidden) and don't have to (not necessary) are not the same. And there is no to after a modal: ✗ I must to go → ✓ I must go.

⚠️ Six phrases that will block you

These are the hardest parts of the article. Tap each one to open it.

MeaningDoing something without a person watching or controlling it.

In contextMuse goes out and does tasks unsupervised — you are not clicking "yes" at every step.

Try it: "Some AI agents run unsupervised for hours."

MeaningNot a standard phrase. The writer creates a name (from Taylor Swift's fans) for one kind of damage.

In contextWhen everyone has a robot that books first and asks later, the demand for a limited resource — tickets, tables, appointments — explodes.

Try it: "Cheap bots create a Swiftie problem for anything in short supply."

Literal meaningCovered with a lot of earth.

In contextA business is buried under messages: it receives far more than it can possibly deal with.

Try it: "The team was buried under support tickets after the launch."

MeaningTo decide to think about something in a particular way, for a practical purpose.

In contextRaskar says: "treat the models like an engine, and the agents like cars". He sets up a comparison to make his rule-making idea clear.

Try it: "Treat the API as a service, not a product."

MeaningThe shared rules that stop people from crashing into each other. Here, used as a metaphor.

In contextThe "road" is the internet, the "cars" are AI agents, and the missing rules are the laws and standards that do not exist yet.

Try it: "Before we ship it, we need rules of the road for who is responsible."

MeaningTo suffer a cost or a loss in order to get something.

In contextUsers get the convenience of agents, but "pay a price" in personal data. A toll also fits the road metaphor.

Try it: "We saved time, but we paid a price in accuracy."

💬 Discussion

  1. Would you let an AI agent read your email and book things for you? What would you allow it to do, and what would you never allow?
  2. Who should be responsible when an agent does something wrong — the user, the company that made it, or the website? Give a reason.
  3. The article says users "pay a price in personal data". Is that a fair price for convenience? Why or why not?
  4. Raskar says we should regulate the agents, not just the models. Why might that be easier — or harder — than regulating the AI itself?

🔧 Activity: Write the Rules of the Road (15 minutes)

Work in small groups. You are the web team for a school club's website.

  1. Write six rules for AI agents that visit your site. Use a different modal in each rule: must, mustn't, have to, don't have to, should, are supposed to.
  2. For each rule, say who the rule is for (the agent, the user, or the website) and why it matters.
  3. Swap lists with another group. Check their grammar: is mustn't really forbidden, or did they mean don't have to?
  4. Choose the three strongest rules and read them aloud to the class.

Extension. Write one sentence that starts "However, ..." to say what might go wrong with your rules. Every good policy has at least one weakness.

🏠 Wrap-up

  • An agent acts; a chatbot only replies. The web was built for humans, not agents.
  • Agents can hoard resources, flood businesses, cut website income, and put your data at risk.
  • The problem is not one tool but a missing set of rules of the road.
  • Modals of obligation (must, have to, should, mustn't, don't have to) are the language of rules.
  • You have now practised all four mid-term question types: reading, rephrasing, vocabulary and grammar.
