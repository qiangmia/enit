This week: AI companies claim their tools can replace human labour. This week you will read a data story about which jobs are most exposed, learn to separate measured facts from predicted ones, and practise the conditionals and hedging words that make a claim honest.

Before you read: Think of one task in your daily life that a machine already does for you. Did it replace anything, or did it change what you do with the time?

🎯By the end of this week, you will be able to
  • separate tasks from jobs when reading about automation;
  • read numbers with their caveats: who measured what, and who disagrees;
  • use first and second conditionals to talk about likely and imagined futures;
  • hedge a claim with may, might, could and is likely to;
  • explain what a benchmark can and cannot tell you.

Read: Will Your Job Be Replaced by AI?

Read along: tap ▶ for narration. Tap a highlighted word for its definition.

Artificial-intelligence companies are making large claims about their tools. Some jobs, they say, will be automated completely. Others will be augmented: the person stays, but the work changes. Company bosses are spending enormous sums on these tools, partly because they expect to save money on headcount.

"Flat is the new up," one saying goes: a company whose workforce stays the same size is now considered a success. If even half of this is true, there will be an impact on all of us — and perhaps sooner than we think.

The clearest evidence comes from software. A benchmark measures how much of a task a model can complete, grouped by how long a human expert would take. Three years ago, models could finish tasks that took a person seconds or minutes. The newest models can complete many tasks that take an hour, and some that take longer. Software is only the first field; financial analysis and early legal work show the same pattern at an earlier stage.

What does that mean for jobs? Stanford University analysed four years of employment data. It found a 2.7% fall in employment for workers aged 22 to 25 since ChatGPT became widely used. In the most exposed sectors — finance, software and creative industries — the fall was 12.8%. Not all economists agree: interest-rate rises, they argue, could explain part of it.

There is a second reason for caution. AI use is measured in tokens, small pieces of text. Token use grew enormously in 2026, and some companies set up "token leaderboards" to push staff to use the tools more. Then the bills arrived, and many began to ration access. If virtual workers cost more than human ones, adoption may slow down. That is a real caveat, not a detail: the forecast depends on the price.

So the honest summary is a conditional one. If routine tasks are automated, junior roles will change, and some will disappear. If the costs stay high, the change will be slower than the headlines suggest. Either way, workers who can check, supervise and correct the output will be in a stronger position than workers who can only produce it.

Adapted from “Will your job be replaced by AI? Here are the roles most affected”, BBC News, 22 July 2026. (source).

📐Grammar: First and Second Conditionals (with hedging)

Conditionals let you say how likely something is. Hedging words let you say how sure you are. News writing needs both.

FormExample
If + present, … will …If companies automate routine tasks, junior roles will change.
If + past, … would …If I were a graduate, I would learn to supervise the tools.
may / might / could + verbThe effect might be smaller than the headline.
It is likely that …It is likely that some tasks will disappear.
Unless …Unless the costs fall, companies will ration access.

Key rules

  • First conditional: a real, possible future. If the costs fall, adoption will rise.
  • Second conditional: imagined or unlikely. If I were a graduate, I would reskill now. Use were for every person.
  • Hedge a claim when the evidence is not final: may, might, could, is likely to, appears to.
  • Unless means if not: Unless the costs fall = If the costs do not fall.
  • Do not use will in the if-clause. The future meaning is already there.

Common mistakes

The classic error is will in both halves: ✗ If AI will replace the task, workers will need new skills. → ✓ If AI replaces the task, workers will need new skills.

The second error is over-claiming. ✗ The study proves that young workers are losing jobs. → ✓ The study suggests that young workers in exposed sectors have been affected.

Activity B: Hedge it

Rewrite each over-claim so that it is honest. Use a conditional and a hedging word.

✗ “AI will replace all junior software jobs.” → ✓ “If companies automate routine coding tasks, some junior roles may change.”
🔁Rephrasing

Hedging is rephrasing with a purpose: the same idea, an honest amount of certainty.

Over-claim → hedged
Original: AI will take these jobs.
Rephrased: AI may change many of these jobs.
Certain → conditional
Original: Young workers are losing jobs.
Rephrased: If the Stanford data is right, young workers in exposed sectors are losing jobs.

Try it

  1. AI will replace all of these jobs.
  2. The numbers prove the effect.
  3. Companies will use it more if the costs fall.
Show model answers
  1. AI may change many of these jobs.
  2. The numbers suggest an effect, but other economists disagree.
  3. If the costs fall, companies will use it more.
