01 Loss of Control & Comprehension
5–10 Year Horizon

The deepest danger is not losing jobs—it is losing the ability to understand our own civilization

In 2026, human beings still hold the reins. Harari’s central warning at the Global Views Summit is that we are approaching a threshold where non-human intelligence becomes vastly smarter and faster than us—and takes over finance, economics, media, and politics before societies learn how to govern it.

Consider the global financial system. Bonds, equities, interest rates, and central bank interventions are already opaque to most citizens. Yet today, a critical mass of economists, central bank governors, and market participants still understands how the machinery works and can steer it during a crisis.

Within ten years, Harari warns, AI could invent financial instruments and trading strategies of mathematical complexity far beyond any human brain. Presidents, prime ministers, central bank chairs, and the world’s wealthiest individuals alike would no longer comprehend what drives capital flows, prices, employment—or even wars sparked by automated systemic shocks.

The labor market offers an early glimpse of this cognitive dislocation. Just five years ago, software programming was widely hailed as the safest bet for young people; today AI is rapidly taking over coding, displacing the vast majority of programmers while leaving only a small tier of elite specialists. Neither universities nor policymakers know what skills to recommend for the next decade.

Key Metaphor

“Humanity risks regressing to the level of a five-year-old child—or like someone with dementia who once understood the world, now facing a reality too complex and chaotic to grasp.”

02 The Industry Contradiction
100:1 Capability vs. Safety

AI leaders say they cannot trust rival humans—yet claim we can trust the “gods” they are creating

Harari emphasizes that the leaders driving the AI revolution are neither evil nor foolish. In fact, many understand the dangers better than outside critics and openly acknowledge them: Elon Musk told The Economist there is a 10% to 20% chance AI could destroy humanity, a warning echoed publicly by Sam Altman and other industry pioneers.

Yet in Harari’s direct conversations with heads of the world’s largest AI companies, he routinely asks two questions that expose a glaring contradiction at the heart of the industry. Why pour roughly 100 times more money into capability than safety—and why assume superintelligent entities will obey human masters indefinitely?

03 The Trust Dilemma & Geopolitics
US · China · Taiwan Crossroads

Geopolitical rivalry traps superpowers in an arms race—while history warns how badly escalation misfires

The crisis is not merely corporate; it escalates directly to geopolitics. Even if all American AI companies agreed to slow down and prioritize safety, executives argue that China would seize the lead. Speak with Chinese counterparts, Harari notes, and you hear the exact mirror image: willingness to regulate, paired with refusal to trust the Americans.

Meanwhile, collapsing geopolitical trust triggers traditional disasters: trade shrinks, wars erupt, and national budgets meant for healthcare and education are diverted into missiles, drones, and tanks. Because nations are trapped in confrontation, they lose the capacity to cooperate on the one transition that requires global rules.

Taiwan stands squarely at this intersection—linking advanced AI technology, the global digital economy, and acute US–China strategic competition. As Harari argues, leaders who launch escalations assuming easy control consistently underestimate history’s complexity.

04 Responsible & Gradual Development
Resilience Principle

Why you cannot test superintelligence the way SpaceX tested early rockets

Harari is explicit: he is not calling for a halt to AI development. Stopping is neither realistic nor desirable, because AI holds transformative positive potential—to revolutionize healthcare, improve transportation, and help humanity confront climate change.

What history teaches, however, is that every human institution—nations, corporations, and individuals—inevitably makes mistakes. Responsible AI development therefore requires gradual pacing and structural resilience: designing safeguards from the outset so errors can be detected, understood, corrected, and contained before cascading out of control.

05 Democratic Agency & Core Message
Not Inevitable

Nothing is predetermined: citizens can still bring AI back under political choice

When asked in The Economist whether he would board a plane with a 20% chance of crashing and killing everyone aboard, Elon Musk replied that the analogy fails because AI is “inevitable”—as if humanity has no choice whether to get on the plane.

Harari firmly rejects this fatalism. Every forecast of our AI future describes a possibility, not destiny. One encouraging signal is already emerging: in the upcoming U.S. midterm elections, AI has entered local political debate for the first time, with candidates pressed to state their positions on AI governance. “That is what politics is for,” Harari notes.

Harari’s stated mission is not to make decisions on behalf of tech executives, but to equip the public with clear knowledge of the stakes—so citizens everywhere can participate in the most consequential decision in human history.

Core Question

“Can humanity continue developing increasingly powerful AI while still understanding and controlling the systems that shape human society?”

AI is no longer solely an engineering challenge. It is a political, social, and civilizational choice centered on four pillars: control, trust, responsibility, and humanity’s freedom to choose its own future.
