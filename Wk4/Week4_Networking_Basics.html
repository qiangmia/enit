Full Text: "When the Network Goes Down"

🔊 Listen to this article

Tap any underlined term in the article to reveal its meaning. All definitions are collected in the glossary below the text.

When the Network Goes Down
A real-world troubleshooting scenario in a Taiwanese office
Name: Lin Zhaohui (林昭輝) Age: 28 Job: Network Administrator
Company: A financial services company, Taipei Education: M.S. in Computer Science

7:52 AM — The Warning

An automated alert is waiting in Zhaohui's inbox: core switch — degraded performance. The dashboard is showing latency climbing past 120 milliseconds and packet loss above two percent. The same alert appeared yesterday morning, and Zhaohui dismissed it after a quick glance, because the dashboard recovered on its own. This morning he is regretting that decision. He is opening the monitoring console, and he is wondering whether he should have escalated it last night.

8:30 AM — The Outage

By the time Zhaohui reaches his desk, nothing is improving. The core network is down. Nobody is reaching the internal systems, and the trading floor is the worst affected: forty traders are watching prices they cannot act on. Every minute of outage is costing the firm money.

Trading floor

Trader: "My screen shows the price, but I can't place a single order. Am I trading or just watching?"

Zhaohui: "You're watching. I'm switching to the incident channel now, and then I'm walking down to the server room."

His manager is standing behind him. "We're completely down," the manager says. "What is taking so long?" Zhaohui is not answering straight away. He is taking a breath, because guessing out loud is how outages become longer.

8:45 AM — Diagnosis

Zhaohui is starting with the simplest test. He is pinging the core router from the server room, and there is no response at all. He is checking the switch, and four ports are showing red. He is reading the firewall logs, and thousands of connection attempts are arriving every minute — far more than a normal Monday morning.

He is triaging now, which means he is sorting what he knows from what he is only guessing at. The physical link is up, because the port lights are on. But the traffic is saturating the link, so real traffic is losing the argument with the junk. Something is flooding the connection, and he cannot yet prove what.

9:15 AM — Containment

Zhaohui is containing the damage before he understands the cause. He is taking the trading floor off the main network entirely. He is moving critical operations across to the backup link, and the company is finally using the redundancy it has been paying for — this is the failover everyone hoped would never be needed.

He is escalating the incident to the security team, who are now analysing the traffic patterns. He is rewriting the firewall rules to block the suspicious addresses. He is resetting the credentials that may have been exposed, and he is scheduling a patch for the old service account that should have been deleted months ago.

Meanwhile, the traders are working from mobile hotspots, sending orders one at a time. It is slower, and it is miserable, but it is working — which is the whole point of redundancy.

11:00 AM — Resolution

By eleven — two and a half hours after the trading floor went dark — the network is stabilising. The security team has identified a brute-force attack against the old service account, and a sudden spike in traffic that overwhelmed the link. A switch port has also been misconfigured since a firmware update the previous evening, so the traffic was never being distributed the way it should have been.

Zhaohui is restoring the services one at a time, and the trading floor is coming back online. He is writing the post-mortem while the room is still hot, because everyone is busy and nobody remembers details well enough to write them down properly later.

That Afternoon — What Changes

Nobody was hurt, and no data was lost, so the post-mortem is unusually calm. The team is documenting the single point of failure that let one bad port stop the whole floor, and they are recommending that the backup link be tested monthly instead of assumed. The backup link had worked for three years without anyone checking. It was not broken — but it had never actually been used, and today it was carrying more traffic than it was ever tested for.

The manager is asking who approved the firmware update. Zhaohui is explaining that the answer is everyone and no one, which is why the recommendation is not about blame. It is about building systems that fail softly, and about noticing the degraded signals a week before they become an emergency.

"Network downtime is not just a technical problem — it's a business problem. Every minute of downtime costs money and customer trust. But the minute before it, someone was looking at a warning and deciding it wasn't important." — — Lin Zhaohui, Network Administrator
Grammar in the Text: Present Continuous

Zhaohui is doing all of this right now, so the story is written almost entirely in the present continuous. As you read, notice how often you see:

  • is / am / are + -ing — "He is checking the switch."
  • for actions in progress at the moment of speaking — "The traders are working from mobile hotspots."
  • for temporary states that started in the past — "The network is stabilising."
  • for changes over time — "Latency is climbing."

Try describing your own morning to a partner using only the present continuous.

Glossary (16 terms)

Every underlined term in the article is defined here. You can also tap the term in the article to jump to its meaning.

outage 服務中斷 — a period of time when a service is completely unavailable. "The outage lasted two hours."
degraded 效能劣化的 — working, but slower or worse than normal. Unlike an outage, users can often still work. See also degrade (verb).
redundancy / redundant 冗餘；冗餘的 — having more than one way to do something, so a failure does not stop everything. "The backup line is a redundancy."
failover 故障轉移 — the automatic or manual switch to a backup system when the main one fails.
single point of failure 單點故障 — one component that, if it breaks, brings down a whole system.
misconfiguration / misconfigured 設定錯誤 — when something is set up wrongly, so it does not behave as intended.
packet loss 封包遺失 — data that never arrives. A small amount is normal; a lot means a bad connection.
saturate / saturated 飽和；使飽和 — to fill a link completely, so nothing more can get through. "The uplink is saturated."
spike 流量暴衝 — a sudden, very large increase. "There was a spike in traffic at 9 a.m."
triage / triaging 分流；初步判讀 — to sort problems by how serious and urgent they are, so you handle the worst first.
containment / contain 圍堵；控制 — stopping a problem from spreading before you fix the cause. "We are focusing on containment first."
escalate 升級處理；向上呈報 — to pass a problem to someone with more authority or expertise. (The noun is escalation.)
brute-force attack 暴力破解攻擊 — trying every possible password until one works.
credentials 帳號憑證 — the username and password (or key) used to prove who you are.
patch 修補 — a fix for a known problem in software. (A security patch closes a vulnerability.)
post-mortem 事後檢討 — a written review after an incident, looking at what happened and what to change. It is about learning, not blame.

Quick Review: Present Continuous Formation

Use am/is/are + verb-ing to describe actions happening now or around now.

PatternExample
I + am + verb-ingI am checking the network status.
He/She/It + is + verb-ingThe server is running slowly.
We/You/They + are + verb-ingThe users are experiencing connectivity issues.
Key Rules
  • For actions happening right now: The network is going down. I'm troubleshooting the problem.
  • For temporary situations: I'm using a mobile hotspot this week.
  • For changing situations: The network is getting slower and slower.
  • Spelling rules: drop -e (make → making), double consonant (sit → sitting)
Common Mistakes (Chinese L1 Speakers)

Chinese speakers: Don't forget the 'be' verb. ✗ I checking the network. → ✓ I am checking the network.
