This week: AI is often described as neutral and objective, but it learns from data — and data can be biased, incomplete, or deliberately poisoned. You will read about the risks, practise skimming for the main idea, scan for details, and simplify difficult sentences.

Before you read: Can a computer program be unfair? What might make an AI system treat one group of people differently from another?

  • simplify long sentences without changing their meaning.

Read: Hidden Bias in AI

The rapid deployment of Artificial Intelligence (AI) across various sectors — from hiring to healthcare and law enforcement — promises greater efficiency and objectivity. However, beneath the algorithms and impressive processing power lies a significant challenge: the risk of perpetuating and amplifying human biases. AI systems are not inherently neutral; they learn from the data they are fed. If that training data reflects existing societal inequalities, the AI will learn and institutionalize those biases.

The primary culprit is biased training data. Imagine an AI hiring tool trained on historical hiring decisions from a company that primarily employed men in leadership roles. This is precisely what happened with a recruiting tool used by a major tech company, which had to be scrapped after it demonstrated clear discrimination. Similarly, in facial recognition technology, studies have frequently shown that AI systems trained predominantly on images of lighter-skinned males exhibit significantly lower accuracy rates when identifying women and people with darker skin tones. In medicine, surgeons also use these models to improve pre-planning, which makes accurate and fair training data even more important.

A second major issue is the “black box” problem. Many complex AI models, particularly deep learning networks, arrive at decisions through processes that are opaque even to their own engineers. This lack of transparency makes it incredibly difficult to audit the systems, pinpoint why a decision was made, or fix a specific source of bias. If an AI unjustly denies a loan application or flags an innocent person as a risk, it is nearly impossible to hold the system or the company accountable if the decision-making logic remains hidden.

To mitigate these risks, the burgeoning field of AI Ethics advocates for principles such as fairness, accountability, transparency, and human oversight. Governments and corporations are now working to establish regulatory frameworks and ethical guidelines. The goal is to ensure that AI serves as a tool for progress rather than a mechanism for social injustice. They aim to create systems that are not just intelligent, but also ethical and equitable for all users.

✂️Language: simplification

Vocabulary simplification replaces a difficult word with a simpler everyday synonym. Sentence simplification breaks a long, complex sentence into shorter sentences while keeping the same meaning.

Vocabulary simplification
Original: “These measures may mitigate the risk.”
Simpler: “These measures may reduce the risk.”
Sentence simplification
Original: “If an AI unjustly denies a loan application, it is nearly impossible to hold the company accountable if the decision-making logic remains hidden.”
Simpler: “An AI may deny a loan unfairly. If we cannot see how it decided, we cannot hold the company responsible.”

Try it

Simplify each sentence. Keep the meaning, but make it easier for a general reader.

  1. “AI systems are not inherently neutral; they learn from the data they are fed.”
  2. “If that training data reflects existing societal inequalities, the AI will learn and institutionalize those biases.”
  3. “This lack of transparency makes it incredibly difficult to audit the systems, pinpoint why a decision was made, or fix a specific source of bias.”
Show model answers
  1. AI is not naturally fair. It learns from the data people give it.
  2. If the training data is unfair, the AI will learn that unfairness and make it part of its normal decisions.
  3. Because the system is not transparent, it is hard to check it, explain a decision, or fix the cause of the bias.
