🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, students will be able to:

  1. Read and comprehend a simplified news article about online kindness.
  2. Understand and use key vocabulary related to social media, crowds, and compassion.
  3. Evaluate whether "kindness trolling" is really a force for good online.
  4. Participate in a structured discussion about online behaviour and responsibility.

The Internet Vigilantes Who Weaponise Kindness
A simplified version of a BBC Future article (September 2026)
Source: BBC Future Level: B1 (Intermediate) Time: ~10 minutes

Every day, Laura Tigeleiro from Staten Island, New York, opened TikTok and saw hatred. "Everyone was making fun of my eye," she says. Tigeleiro has multiple sclerosis, an autoimmune disease that makes one of her eyes droop sideways. "I just wanted to share healthy recipes, and maybe make some friends," she says. "But they said terrible things. It really hurt."

Then one of her videos went viral with more than 15,000 friendly comments. "I didn't know what was going on. I was shocked," says Tigeleiro. Days later, she understood: she was under attack — by the Kindness Mob.

A TikTok channel that sends a large crowd to leave kind comments on a person's posts

The Kindness Mob is a TikTok channel run by Tyler Brickley, a digital marketer from Kansas City, US. He started the project in 2023. "It's a form of trolling," he says. Every day, Brickley points his half-a-million followers at someone who is being bullied or overlooked, and the crowd floods that person's comments with kindness. "We're hijacking the machinery of online harassment — like coordination and anonymity — and using them for benevolence. And I gotta say, it works."

Kindness; the desire to do good (善意)

Brickley says he has organised about 350 mobs. A successful post can get tens of thousands of comments and millions of views. Sometimes famous people join — the celebrity Paris Hilton joined one mob. The kindness often changes people's lives. Renee Penick, a grandmother from Ohio, says the Mob supported her while her husband was in hospital. After the Mob visited Tigeleiro, she gained so many followers that TikTok started paying her — enough to buy her family a much-needed new car.

Researchers say this is an example of "social contagion": behaviour spreads from person to person like a virus. Nicholas Christakis, a professor at Yale University, says humans evolved to be kind as well as cruel. Social media algorithms often highlight conflict to keep us watching, but the Kindness Mob encourages a more natural, pleasant kind of interaction. His research shows that kindness can create cascades: you are kind to Susie, Susie is kind to Betty, and Betty is kind to Tommy.

The spreading of behaviour or feelings between people
A chain reaction, where one kind act leads to many more

But the work is not easy. Brickley says 20 to 100 people ask for help every day, but he can only post one mob a day. "I have to turn 19 of them away," he says. The burden became so heavy that he had to take a few months off. In August, he made only $39 from TikTok. Professor Alice Marwick, who has studied internet culture for two decades, says such projects "have a very limited shelf life" — the people behind them often get burned out within a year or two.

Still, Brickley continues. After he had back surgery, his wife secretly posted a video of him. He woke up and read 1,000 kind comments from his own mob — and cried. "It feels like a calling," he says. "The Kindness Mob is just one step. But kindness, for everyone, always, feels like a good place to start."

💬 Discussion Questions

Discuss these questions with a partner or small group. Be ready to share your ideas with the class.

  1. Is "kindness trolling" really a form of trolling? Can a crowd of 500,000 people be both supportive and frightening at the same time?
  2. Should strangers be able to flood your account with comments — even kind ones? What are the good and bad sides of this?
  3. Why do algorithms often show us conflict and hate? How does this affect the way we feel when we open our phones?
  4. Would you join the Kindness Mob? What would a "good internet" look like to you?

🎭 Activity: Is Weaponised Kindness Good?

Work in groups of 4. Each group defends a different point of view. Prepare arguments and share them with the class.

💛
Group A: Kindness Mob
Kindness is the most powerful weapon. We stop bullying without any violence.
📱
Group B: Targets
We are grateful, but suddenly being watched by 500,000 people feels strange.
🔬
Group C: Researchers
Kindness is contagious, but one person cannot carry the burden. Platforms must do more.
⚖️
Group D: Skeptics
A mob is still a mob — even a kind one. It can become a new type of online bullying.

Instructions

  1. Prepare (5 minutes): Discuss with your group. Write 2-3 arguments for your point of view.
  2. Present (2 minutes per group): Share your arguments with the class.
  3. Debate (5 minutes): Ask questions and respond to other groups.
  4. Vote (3 minutes): The class votes on the most convincing point of view.
Example Arguments

Group A (Kindness Mob): "A troll uses negativity to hurt people. We use the same tools, but for good. Kindness wins."

Group D (Skeptics): "If the crowd can decide who deserves attention, who decides who gets attacked? Power without rules is dangerous."

After class, think about these questions:

  1. Have you ever seen someone being bullied online? How did you react? What could you do differently?
  2. If you started a "kindness mob" at your school, what would it look like? Who would you help first?
  3. Find one post or article about online kindness (in English). Bring it to class next week to discuss.
